In the morning Maelle went down to the yard. No one stopped her. A hostage was a guest, the warden had said, and guests could walk where they liked, so long as they did not try to leave the pass. The riders saddling their dragons for the morning patrol watched her cross the yard with the polite, careful faces people use for someone they expect to see hanged.

In daylight Maelle could see her properly: grey-gold scales gone dull at the edges, a long scar across her jaw, and the wing, that terrible wing, lying across the stones like a torn sail. Near the wall, on an upturned bucket, a man sat mending a leather harness. He had one arm. He worked the awl with his teeth and his single hand, slowly, as if he had all the time in the world.

That, one of the riders called across the yard, is Hallam. He mends wings. Or he used to, before his dragon died and he forgot how. The others laughed. Hallam did not look up.

Maelle went and stood beside him. She asked the dragon's name. Veyra, he said. Her rider went over the rocks in a storm three winters ago. She tried to catch him and tore herself open doing it. She burns anyone who touches the wing now. We have tried tar and sail needles as thick as your finger. The warden says that if she is still grounded in spring, they will put her down.

Maelle looked at the wing for a long time. Then she said, very quietly, You have been trying to patch it. It is not a sail. It is more like silk. It tears along the grain, see there, and there. If you pull the edges together with a heavy needle, you make new holes, and every hole is a new pain. It needs to be drawn together, not forced.

Hallam put the awl down and looked at her properly for the first time. He looked at her face, and then at her hands, at the calluses on her fingertips and the small white scars across her knuckles that no lady's maid would ever have allowed. He only asked what she would need.

She showed him her mother's bone needle case. Inside lay twelve needles, fine as hair, and a spool of waxed silk thread.

They waited until dusk, when the riders were at supper and the yard was empty. Maelle walked out across the snow alone. Hallam stood by the wall with a bucket of water that would be no use at all. Veyra watched her come. The amber eye did not blink. Heat rose off the great body in slow waves, and Maelle could feel it on her face from twenty paces away.

She did not go to the long tear. She went to the wingtip, where there was a small rent no longer than her hand, and she knelt beside it in the snow. She threaded the finest needle. She spoke to the dragon the way she had once spoken to frightened children at their first fitting: telling her exactly what she was going to do before she did it. Then she drew the first stitch.

Veyra's head came round. The jaws opened a little. Maelle could see the glow deep in the throat, and she kept stitching, because stopping halfway would hurt more. Seven stitches. Eight. Nine. She tied off the thread and cut it with her teeth. The tear was closed.

The dragon lowered her head until the scarred jaw rested on the snow, very close to Maelle's knees, and breathed out a long, warm breath that smelled of smoke. Behind them, by the wall, Hallam let out the breath he had been holding. And from the dark doorway of the barracks, a rider who had come back early for his gloves stood watching, and then turned and walked quickly toward the warden's tower.
