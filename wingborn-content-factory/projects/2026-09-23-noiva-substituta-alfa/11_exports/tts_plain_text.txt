On the morning the hostage was due to leave for the dragon citadel, Maelle's stepmother came down to the sewing room herself. Odrenne never came down to the sewing room. She stood in the doorway in her grey silk, looked at the girl bent over the hem of a curtain, her mother's bone needle case open at her elbow, and said, You will go north today. You will go as Isolde. And if you ever tell anyone your real name, your father's grave will be dug up and emptied into the river.

Maelle was twenty-six years old. She had been the seamstress of House Varrow for eight years, ever since her father died and his widow decided that a stepdaughter was cheaper than a servant. She had mended every gown Isolde owned. She knew the exact width of Isolde's shoulders. That, it turned out, was why she had been chosen.

They dressed her in Isolde's travelling cloak and Isolde's gloves. They took away her apron and her sewing basket. The only thing she was allowed to keep was the needle case, because Odrenne did not think it worth the trouble of taking. Maelle slipped it into her sleeve and held it there, all the way down the stairs, the way a child holds a hand.

The treaty was older than anyone living. Every generation, a daughter of House Varrow went to live for one year at Cold Reach, the citadel carved into the northern pass, where the dragon riders kept the road between the kingdoms. The hostage was a promise. As long as Varrow kept faith, the girl was treated as a guest. Everyone at the house spoke of it as an honour. Isolde had cried for three days when her name was drawn.

The carriage climbed north for six days, into snow. On the seventh morning the road turned, and Maelle saw the citadel for the first time: towers cut straight into the grey rock, and above them, circling slowly in the cold air, the dark shapes of dragons. They were larger than she had imagined, and quieter, and they did not look at her at all.

The warden of Cold Reach met her in the gate hall. He was a tall man with a lined, tired face, and he bowed to her as if she were a lady. Then he read her the terms of the treaty aloud, as the law required. She listened to the familiar words about guests and honour and faith. And then he read a line that no one at Varrow had ever spoken in her hearing. Should House Varrow break the treaty, the hostage shall answer for it at the gate, by the old law of the pass.

Maelle understood, standing there in her stepsister's cloak, exactly why Isolde had cried. And she understood something colder. Odrenne did not send people places by accident. If she had sent the stepdaughter nobody would miss, it was because she already knew the treaty was going to break.

The warden asked if she had any questions. Maelle said no, my lord, in Isolde's careful voice, and he nodded, and a servant led her up a long stair to a room with a narrow window and a good fire. She sat on the bed with the needle case in her lap and did not cry. She was trying to count the days until spring.

That night a sound woke her, a long low cry that shook the glass. She went to the window. Below, in a yard of trampled snow, an old dragon lay alone, grey-gold in the torchlight, one enormous wing spread across the stones. The wing was torn from shoulder to tip. Two riders stood well back from her with spears. And the dragon lifted her head, turned one amber eye toward the lit window, and looked straight at Maelle.

In the morning Maelle went down to the yard. No one stopped her. A hostage was a guest, the warden had said, and guests could walk where they liked, so long as they did not try to leave the pass. The riders saddling their dragons for the morning patrol watched her cross the yard with the polite, careful faces people use for someone they expect to see hanged.

In daylight Maelle could see her properly: grey-gold scales gone dull at the edges, a long scar across her jaw, and the wing, that terrible wing, lying across the stones like a torn sail. Near the wall, on an upturned bucket, a man sat mending a leather harness. He had one arm. He worked the awl with his teeth and his single hand, slowly, as if he had all the time in the world.

That, one of the riders called across the yard, is Hallam. He mends wings. Or he used to, before his dragon died and he forgot how. The others laughed. Hallam did not look up.

Maelle went and stood beside him. She asked the dragon's name. Veyra, he said. Her rider went over the rocks in a storm three winters ago. She tried to catch him and tore herself open doing it. She burns anyone who touches the wing now. We have tried tar and sail needles as thick as your finger. The warden says that if she is still grounded in spring, they will put her down.

Maelle looked at the wing for a long time. Then she said, very quietly, You have been trying to patch it. It is not a sail. It is more like silk. It tears along the grain, see there, and there. If you pull the edges together with a heavy needle, you make new holes, and every hole is a new pain. It needs to be drawn together, not forced.

Hallam put the awl down and looked at her properly for the first time. He looked at her face, and then at her hands, at the calluses on her fingertips and the small white scars across her knuckles that no lady's maid would ever have allowed. He only asked what she would need.

She showed him her mother's bone needle case. Inside lay twelve needles, fine as hair, and a spool of waxed silk thread.

They waited until dusk, when the riders were at supper and the yard was empty. Maelle walked out across the snow alone. Hallam stood by the wall with a bucket of water that would be no use at all. Veyra watched her come. The amber eye did not blink. Heat rose off the great body in slow waves, and Maelle could feel it on her face from twenty paces away.

She did not go to the long tear. She went to the wingtip, where there was a small rent no longer than her hand, and she knelt beside it in the snow. She threaded the finest needle. She spoke to the dragon the way she had once spoken to frightened children at their first fitting: telling her exactly what she was going to do before she did it. Then she drew the first stitch.

Veyra's head came round. The jaws opened a little. Maelle could see the glow deep in the throat, and she kept stitching, because stopping halfway would hurt more. Seven stitches. Eight. Nine. She tied off the thread and cut it with her teeth. The tear was closed.

The dragon lowered her head until the scarred jaw rested on the snow, very close to Maelle's knees, and breathed out a long, warm breath that smelled of smoke. Behind them, by the wall, Hallam let out the breath he had been holding. And from the dark doorway of the barracks, a rider who had come back early for his gloves stood watching, and then turned and walked quickly toward the warden's tower.

The warden sent for her before breakfast. He sat behind a table covered in maps, and he did not ask her to sit. A rider tells me, he said, that the Lady Isolde of Varrow spent last evening kneeling in the snow beside a dragon who has burned four men, sewing her wing. He tells me the dragon allowed it.

Maelle said yes, my lord. He asked where a lady of Varrow had learned to sew like that. She said her old nurse had taught her. It was the kind of lie that only works if nobody cares enough to check, and she watched him decide, for now, not to care. He looked out of the window at the yard, where Veyra lay with her head raised, watching the tower. If you can close that wing, he said at last, you may try. She is the oldest dragon on the pass. I would rather not put her down.

So Maelle went to work. Every afternoon, while the light lasted, she knelt beside the wing with Hallam at her side. He could not sew, but he knew the wing: where the bones ran under the membrane, where it would bear weight and where it would only pretend to. He held the edges in his one hand and she drew them together with the fine needles from her mother's case, a few inches a day, never more than the dragon could stand. The riders stopped laughing after the first week. By the second, some of them stopped by with hot wine to watch.

On the eighteenth day Veyra lowered her wing for her. She simply laid it down flat across the stones, the long tear turned up toward Maelle's hands, and closed her eyes. That was the day they began the long seam, from shoulder to tip.

On the twentieth day a courier was caught on the pass. When the riders searched him they found a sealed letter under the lining of his saddle. It was addressed to Lord Castellan, whose lands lay on the far side of the mountains. The seal was the grey hawk of House Varrow.

The warden sent for Maelle again that evening. This time he told her to sit. He read the letter to her himself, slowly, and she recognised every turn of phrase, because she had heard that voice through the sewing room door for eight years. The renewal will not be signed. The pass will be yours by the first thaw, as agreed, and my daughter will be your son's wife before midsummer. You need not concern yourself with the girl at Cold Reach. She is not my daughter. She is of no consequence to anyone.

The warden folded the letter. He said, The law of the pass says the hostage of a broken treaty answers for it at the gate. It does not say what happens to a hostage who is not who she claims to be. So I will ask you once, and you will think carefully before you answer. Who are you?

Maelle looked at her hands. She thought of her father's grave, and of Odrenne in the sewing room doorway, and of how easy it would be to keep being Isolde for a few more days. She said, I am the woman who is mending your dragon, my lord. May I finish before I answer you?

The warden looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded. The renewal is in six days, he said. Castellan's riders are already gathering on the far side of the pass. Finish quickly.

She worked by lantern light after that. On the fifth night, with her fingers bleeding and Hallam asleep against the wall, she tied the last knot at the tip of the long seam and cut the thread. Veyra opened her eyes. Slowly, carefully, the old dragon lifted the mended wing and spread it wide against the stars, and held it there, trembling, and did not fly.

The renewal was held at the Cold Reach gate, as it always had been, on a clear, cold morning. The warden waited at a stone table set in the gateway itself, with the treaty scroll unrolled before him. And up the long road from the south, in a black carriage with the grey hawk painted on its door, came Odrenne of Varrow, to renew the treaty in person.

She stepped down in grey silk and furs. She greeted the warden with perfect courtesy. Then she turned, as custom required, to greet the hostage, and for the first time in twenty-six days she looked Maelle full in the face. Nothing moved in Odrenne's expression. Only her eyes went, very briefly, to Maelle's hands, and then to the yard behind her, where an old dragon lay watching with her wing folded against her side.

The warden asked House Varrow to set its seal to the renewal. Odrenne smiled, and said that House Varrow had considered the matter carefully, and would not. And as the words left her mouth, a horn sounded from the far side of the pass.

They came over the eastern ridge in a long dark line: Castellan's riders, twenty dragons or more, flying low and fast toward the gate. The riders of Cold Reach ran for their mounts. The warden stood, and his face was grey, and he said the words the law required of him. The treaty is broken. The hostage will answer for it at the gate. Two riders took Maelle by the arms and walked her to the centre of the gateway, into the open, where every dragon in the sky could see her. Odrenne stepped back toward her carriage and did not look at her again.

Maelle stood in the cold wind and watched the dark line come on. She was not thinking about Odrenne, or Isolde, or the law of the pass. She was thinking about the long seam, and whether the knots at the tip would hold.

Behind her, in the yard, something enormous moved.

Veyra rose onto her feet. She spread both wings, the whole one and the mended one, and the gold light of the morning poured through the new seam like light through a church window. She beat them once, and the snow of the yard lifted in a white wave. She beat them again, and she was in the air. She flew low over the gate, so low that her shadow passed over Maelle's face, and she went straight at the dark line of Castellan's riders, alone, roaring, with fire in her throat.

The first of the enemy dragons broke away. Then the next. Castellan's riders had been told the old dragon of Cold Reach would never fly again. Within minutes the dark line was scattering back over the eastern ridge, and Veyra was circling slowly above the gate, over the one person she had chosen to defend.

The warden walked out into the gateway. He took Odrenne's letter from his coat, and in front of both houses, and every rider on the wall, he read it aloud, all the way to the last line. She is not my daughter. She is of no consequence to anyone.

Then he turned to Maelle. I asked you once, he said. I am asking you again, here, where everyone can hear. By the law of the pass, a hostage who is not who she claims to be has no protection under the treaty. Think carefully. Who are you?

Maelle looked at Odrenne. She thought of her father's grave, and of the sewing room door, and of eight years of being no one. Then she looked up at the dragon circling in the clear sky, on a wing she had closed stitch by stitch with her own hands.

My name is Maelle, she said. I am the daughter of Aldren of Varrow. I have never been Isolde. And I am not of no consequence.

For a long moment no one on the wall spoke. Then Odrenne laughed, lightly, the way she laughed at guests who used the wrong fork. She is a seamstress, she said. She has been confused for years. I sent her north as a kindness.

The warden did not look at her. He looked at the letter in his hand, and then at the dragon circling above the gate, and then at Maelle. The law of the pass, he said, is older than any house. It says the hostage answers for the treaty. It was written to stop great houses from breaking their word. It was never written to let a great house send someone else to die for its lies. House Varrow has broken the treaty twice today, once with its seal and once with its hostage. Varrow will answer for both.

Two riders stepped forward and took the reins of the black carriage. Odrenne's smile went very still. She would travel south under guard, the warden said, to answer before the king's court for selling the pass. Lord Castellan's letters would go with her. There would be no marriage for Isolde before midsummer, or after it.

Then he turned back to Maelle, and his voice changed. As for you, he said. You came here under a false name, and you could have kept it. Today it would have cost you your life, and you gave it up anyway. The pass needs a keeper, and a keeper is chosen by what she does for the pass, not by the house she comes from. Veyra has chosen you already. I will only write it down.

The riders along the wall did something then that Maelle would remember for the rest of her life. One by one, they drew their gloves off and held up their bare hands, the old salute of the pass, the salute for someone who has worked with their hands to keep the road. Hallam was at the end of the line. He had only one hand to raise, and he raised it highest of all.

Veyra came down out of the sky and landed in the gateway beside her, so gently that the snow barely stirred. The mended wing folded against her side. Maelle put her palm flat against the seam, and the old dragon closed her eyes.

Odrenne was helped into her carriage. At the door she turned and looked back, and for a moment it seemed she might say something. Maelle waited, the way she had waited in the sewing room doorway for eight years. But she found she no longer needed to hear it. Whatever Odrenne might say, it would not change what she had done with her own hands. She turned away first.

She did not go back to Varrow. That spring the warden sent riders south, and they brought her father's remains home to the pass, where no one could threaten to dig them up again. She laid him beneath a stone above the gate, where the wind was clean and the view ran all the way to the south.

Hallam became wing-master of Cold Reach again. Together, over the years that followed, he and Maelle taught a dozen young riders how to read a torn wing by its grain, and how to draw it closed without forcing it. Every one of them learned on the fine needles from the bone needle case, and every one of them was told whose they had been.

And on quiet evenings, when the patrols were in and the fires were lit, the Keeper of the Pass sat by the gate with her mending in her lap, while an old grey-gold dragon slept beside her in the snow. When travellers asked her name, she told them. Maelle, she would say. Just Maelle. And it was enough.
