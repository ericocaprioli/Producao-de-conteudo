On the morning the hostage was due to leave for the dragon citadel, Maelle's stepmother came down to the sewing room herself. Odrenne never came down to the sewing room. She stood in the doorway in her grey silk, looked at the girl bent over the hem of a curtain, her mother's bone needle case open at her elbow, and said, You will go north today. You will go as Isolde. And if you ever tell anyone your real name, your father's grave will be dug up and emptied into the river.

Maelle was twenty-six years old. She had been the seamstress of House Varrow for eight years, ever since her father died and his widow decided that a stepdaughter was cheaper than a servant. She had mended every gown Isolde owned. She knew the exact width of Isolde's shoulders. That, it turned out, was why she had been chosen.

They dressed her in Isolde's travelling cloak and Isolde's gloves. They took away her apron and her sewing basket. The only thing she was allowed to keep was the needle case, because Odrenne did not think it worth the trouble of taking. Maelle slipped it into her sleeve and held it there, all the way down the stairs, the way a child holds a hand.

The treaty was older than anyone living. Every generation, a daughter of House Varrow went to live for one year at Cold Reach, the citadel carved into the northern pass, where the dragon riders kept the road between the kingdoms. The hostage was a promise. As long as Varrow kept faith, the girl was treated as a guest. Everyone at the house spoke of it as an honour. Isolde had cried for three days when her name was drawn.

The carriage climbed north for six days, into snow. On the seventh morning the road turned, and Maelle saw the citadel for the first time: towers cut straight into the grey rock, and above them, circling slowly in the cold air, the dark shapes of dragons. They were larger than she had imagined, and quieter, and they did not look at her at all.

The warden of Cold Reach met her in the gate hall. He was a tall man with a lined, tired face, and he bowed to her as if she were a lady. Then he read her the terms of the treaty aloud, as the law required. She listened to the familiar words about guests and honour and faith. And then he read a line that no one at Varrow had ever spoken in her hearing. Should House Varrow break the treaty, the hostage shall answer for it at the gate, by the old law of the pass.

Maelle understood, standing there in her stepsister's cloak, exactly why Isolde had cried. And she understood something colder. Odrenne did not send people places by accident. If she had sent the stepdaughter nobody would miss, it was because she already knew the treaty was going to break.

The warden asked if she had any questions. Maelle said no, my lord, in Isolde's careful voice, and he nodded, and a servant led her up a long stair to a room with a narrow window and a good fire. She sat on the bed with the needle case in her lap and did not cry. She was trying to count the days until spring.

That night a sound woke her, a long low cry that shook the glass. She went to the window. Below, in a yard of trampled snow, an old dragon lay alone, grey-gold in the torchlight, one enormous wing spread across the stones. The wing was torn from shoulder to tip. Two riders stood well back from her with spears. And the dragon lifted her head, turned one amber eye toward the lit window, and looked straight at Maelle.
